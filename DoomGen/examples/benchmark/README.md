# DoomGen Benchmark Scenarios

This directory contains several benchmark scenarios for Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), designed to test different aspects of agent coordination, navigation, and combat.

## Scenario Overviews

### Ammo Carrier (`ammo_carrier.py`)
This scenario is a fixed-role asymmetric defense task. Player 1 is the stationary "Defender" locked in the center with a chaingun and limited ammo, while the remaining players are fast unarmed "Runners" who must fetch ammo from outlying depots and bring it back. Carrier handoff is automatic once a runner reaches the defender, so the learning problem is not button-precise transfer execution but when to restock, how much to carry, and how to time supply runs under enemy pressure. Successful gameplay requires constant communication and efficient delivery cycles to keep the Defender from being overwhelmed by waves of enemies. It tests asymmetric coordination, resource management, and whether runners learn to relieve shared ammo bottlenecks without being steered by strict positional reward shaping.

### Armory Siege (`armory_siege.py`)
In this defensive scenario, players are tasked with protecting a central "Defense Core" entity from relentless waves of melee-based monsters. The environment includes a central hub connected via corridors to specialized supply rooms for weapons, ammunition, and medical supplies. The doors to the supply rooms are time-gated, so agents have to be careful when making runs not to get stuck leaving the core helpless. Players must balance defending the core with making risky supply runs to upgrade their arsenal and restore health. It primarily tests combat skill, area control, and the strategic management of supply lines. It also test whether agents can swap roles betweeen defending and rushing.

### Rhythm Sync (`rhythm_sync.py`)
This scenario is a progressive rhythm-synchronization benchmark where two agents are separated into parallel rooms and cannot exchange explicit messages. Each stage contains wall switches that must be pressed simultaneously within a strict tolerance window; pressing alone immediately fails the episode. The curriculum is staged deliberately: agents first learn a fixed shared beat with mostly stable switch placement, then a jittered beat with randomized switch placement, and finally a silent-partner variant where only one agent receives the beat cue while the cue owner alternates across stages. The environment exposes lightweight RL telemetry for stage type, cue ownership, pending-switch state, and relative switch position so the benchmark measures cooperative timing and convention formation rather than HUD OCR. It tests implicit coordination, temporal prediction, and adaptation to partial observability.

### Lava Maze (`lava_maze.py`)
Lava Maze is an asymmetric cooperative mission where one agent navigates a procedural grid of pillars over a lava pit while another agent watches from a high-altitude spectator stand. The navigator has an obstructed view and must rely on the spectator's ability to trigger light signals (color-coded flashes) to determine the safe path. The maze is dynamic, with pillars rising or sinking based on the navigator's position and the spectator's inputs. This tests asymmetric communication, spatial reasoning, and trust between agents.

### Lava Pit (`lavapit.py`)
A fully cooperative bridge-handoff traversal benchmark over a bright lava sea. The shipped benchmark keeps a reproducible route geometry by default, but the active plate pair on later handoffs still rerolls every episode from safe candidate footprints, so agents cannot solve it by memorizing a single exact bridge script. The first three bridge handoffs are intentionally easier bootstrap bridges: they stay straighter, use shorter gaps, face the spawn direction, and use deterministic bridge-line red/green plates so the first affordance is always clearly present. The very first handoff is an explicit discovery scaffold: the first two platforms are oversized, the first gap is clamped shorter, the first green/red hold plates are enlarged and anchored at the bridge-facing lip, and bridge 0 keeps a short support latch after activation so random early movement reveals “hold here while the partner crosses.” This lowers the exploration burden enough for agents to discover the core convention before the benchmark widens into the randomized handoff pattern.

Each bridge is controlled by paired entry/exit pressure plates, and later bridges reroll the active plate location every episode from a small safe set that stays on the bridge-facing half and away from platform edges. One agent must keep the bridge supported from the origin side while the partner crosses, then the partner must take over support from the far-side exit plate so the holder can follow. Unsupported bridge occupancy ends in a lava fall, so solo “tap the plate and dash” play is not a valid solution. Progress is measured by jointly cleared bridges, and the episode only succeeds once all active players reach the final platform.

Canonical shaping should stay monotone and centered on team completion, but the benchmark is allowed a small frontier curriculum signal because the core cooperative skill is a transient handoff, not a static room transition. Reward only one-time ordered frontier milestones within the currently active handoff: origin-side support engaged, outbound bridge occupancy, far-side support takeover, return bridge occupancy, and then joint platform completion. Those milestone rewards must reset only when the team's joint best platform advances, and success/failure outcomes must still dominate partial bootstrap progress. Raw x/y-forward reward, `USER12` lead-platform reward, reversible plate-toggling bonuses, repeated occupancy rewards, or rewards for arbitrary camping on the next platform are non-canonical because they over-credit one-sided advance instead of reciprocal bridge conversion. `true_objective` should stay jointly cleared bridges (`joint_best_platform - 1`), not shaped return or lead progress. It tests reciprocal assistance, temporary role asymmetry, and whether agents can convert split handoff states into monotone joint progress without exploiting dense shaping.

### Platform Chain (`platform_chain.py`)
Inspired by the game *Chained Together*, this scenario features a serpentine ascent of 48 levels by default where players are physically linked by a virtual chain. Agents must coordinate their movement to stay within a maximum distance or risk being snapped back, with a HUD tether overlay indicating stable, tight, or dragging chain states. The ascent is no longer a mostly fixed curriculum: ACS rerolls the safe opening stretch each episode and randomizes later platform archetypes and motion phases using `Random(...)`, so agents cannot just memorize the first few easy jumps and one canonical hazard ordering. The vertical route still mixes moving polyobject panels, lifts, and lethal vertical crushers, but their placement and timing now vary run-to-run while preserving a fair warmup and the tethered long-horizon coordination challenge. The scenario also renders the chain as in-world marker actors interpolated between linked players instead of using the HUD overlay. It is useful when you want the tether readable in gameplay videos or from external viewpoints rather than just from the active player HUD.

### Smart Enemies (`smart_enemies.py`)
Set in a circular arena with damaging toxic floors, this scenario focuses on combat against enemies with adaptive AI behavior. These "hunted" demons change their speed and aggression levels based on the proximity and density of the players, punishing many agents attacking the same enemy at once. Players must manage their health and ammo by looting defeated enemies while avoiding environmental damage and navigating obstacles. It tests dynamic combat adaptation, team spacing, and tactical prioritization.

### Dumb Enemies (`dumb_enemies.py`)
Unlike Smart Enemies, this scenario focuses on the exact reverse behavior dynamics. The fleeing enemies will sprint incredibly fast away from players if they are being chased by only one person, but they will drastically slow down to a normal speed if two or more players approach them, encouraging players to hunt and corner enemies together. It tests the agents' ability to coordinate attacks and maintain team proximity.

### Stealth Labyrinth (`stealth_labyrinth.py`)
An asymmetric Torch/Gunner benchmark built around tightly coupled movement, joint evasion, and dependent combat rather than puzzle-holding mechanics. Player 1 ("The Torch") cannot attack at all and instead only lights up nearby rooms and corridors; Player 2 ("The Gunner") carries a `Chaingun` and is effectively blind in the dark. The default map is a simple start tunnel feeding a central hub with a single active branch and two chained rooms, though both the number of branches and the number of rooms per branch are configurable.

When `rooms_per_branch == 1`, each branch behaves like the simple baseline and contains one stationary `SLTurretGuard`. When branches have multiple rooms, each room contains a small stationary enemy group of one or two `SLTurretGuard`s. These groups progress sequentially within the branch: only the front-most uncleared room is spawned into active play, and deeper room enemies are spawned only after the earlier room is fully cleared. The gunner still cannot damage the currently active enemies unless the Torch is close enough to light that room or its approach corridor.

The shipped defaults intentionally include a bootstrap room. The first active room places its first turret closer to the entrance, gives it reduced health, and uses a softer barrage than the deeper rooms so agents can more easily discover the required “Torch steps up, Gunner shoots through light, both dodge together” convention before later rooms demand longer execution. Canonical shaping should stay monotone: keep `true_objective = destroyed_enemies / total_enemies`, give a larger one-time bonus for first-room discovery than later room discoveries, keep large one-time kill rewards, and penalize shared damage/death. Do not reward reversible behaviors such as re-entering lit rooms, camping in the corridor, or merely staying close together without clearing enemies.

The episode succeeds when all enemies are destroyed and fails as soon as either player dies. Projectile hits deal meaningful but not instantly overwhelming damage, so repeated mistakes are punished without turning the benchmark into one-hit roulette. The environment exposes lightweight RL telemetry for both players' alive state, team distance, enemies remaining, whether any live target is currently illuminated, enemies destroyed, total enemies, unique combat rooms discovered by the team, and shared team health. `true_objective` should stay purely `destroyed_enemies / total_enemies`. Canonical shaping can add only monotone one-time room-discovery bonuses, large one-time kill rewards, and shared damage/death penalties. It should not reward reversible behaviors such as re-entering the same room, camping in lit zones, or merely staying close together without actually clearing enemies.

### Foraging Commons (`foraging_commons.py`)
A classic Harvest social dilemma inspired by common-pool resource games. By default, two agents share a large open harvest field (1600×1600 units), though the generator can scale up to four players. Health bonuses spawn at a rate governed by a global `spawn_rate` variable (clamped to 5–100, starts at 80). Each pickup gives +2 HP and degrades the spawn rate by 2, and the rate also decays naturally by 2 over time, so over-harvesting collapses the commons materially faster than before. To restore it, an agent must travel to a distinct Cleanup Station (384×384, NUKAGE floor) farther south of the field and remain there until the cleanup counter completes, which takes about 72 tics in practice (roughly 2 seconds with the current 4-tic polling loop), boosting the rate by 15 with no direct health reward. Agents lose 2 HP per second continuously and must harvest to survive; if any tracked agent starves, the episode ends. Spawn attempts scale with the number of active players. The scenario also exposes commons telemetry for RL wrappers: shared spawn rate, shared harvest/cleanup totals, and per-agent harvest, cleanup, and cleanup-progress counters. A top-mounted HUD bar displays the current spawn rate. It tests public-goods provision, tragedy-of-the-commons dynamics, and the emergence of cooperator/free-rider roles. Have to leave and re-enter cleanup space to do cleanup again to avoid spamming

### Stag Hunt Arena (`stag_hunt_arena.py`)
A direct embodiment of the Stag Hunt coordination dilemma. Two hunters share an arena where weak "Rabbits" (1 HP, frightened, Speed 24, A_Wander scatter behavior) spawn every 20 tics at perimeter and interior locations, capped at 12 alive. Rabbits are only vulnerable inside a close-range kill radius around the hunters, so long-range potshots do not trivialize the hare option, and their corpses are cleaned up automatically after a short delay. Each rabbit can still be killed solo for minor reward (+3 HP). A massive "Stag" (Baron-class, 500 HP) spawns periodically at map center. The Stag is invulnerable unless *both* players are within 800 units simultaneously — when shielded, it regenerates 35 HP/sec, making solo attempts futile. Both must converge and commit to earn the large cooperative reward (+50 HP and +20 shells each). The Stag despawns after ~70 seconds if not killed, preventing endless standoffs. Players wield Shotguns with effectively unlimited shells (9999 capacity/start ammo in the generator). Canonical RL shaping should preserve the dilemma by keeping rabbit outcomes local while sharing only stag-kill outcomes, rather than blanket-sharing all rewards. It tests emergent convention formation, trust building, and whether agents converge on the payoff-dominant or risk-dominant equilibrium.

### Co-op Puzzle (`coop_puzzle.py`)
This benchmark presents a series of paired linear zones where two players are physically separated by blocking windows but can see each other through the partition. On each odd-to-even transition, one highlighted pressure plate becomes active in one lane and opens the relay door for the partner on the opposite side; the following even-to-odd corridor is then open traversal until the next relay gate. Some later odd zones switch to explicit "AND" logic, requiring both players to stand on their respective sync plates together to open both doors. It tests turn-taking, partner-specific door enabling, and periodic synchronized advancement under partial affordance ambiguity.

### Co-op Health Gathering (`coop_health_gathering.py`)
Cooperative health gathering scenario. Players must navigate a toxic maze while chained together. Health items spawn randomly around the map. Game ends if any player dies.
